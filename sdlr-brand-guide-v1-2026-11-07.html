01

How to use this

Read sections 2 through 4 once. Keep sections 5 through 12 open while you work. If something isn't covered here, ask — don't guess, and don't borrow from a competitor.

The one-minute version

SDLR is a design-build firm for outdoor living serving high-end San Diego homes. It is not a landscaping company, and nothing you make should suggest it is. The visual register is Architectural Digest, not a contractor flyer. The reference brands are Rolls-Royce and private yacht charter — luxury with real, warm humans behind it.

"Sell the experience, not the product."

Three rules carry most of the weight. If you remember nothing else:

  1. Restraint reads as money. Cream space, one accent, one idea per piece. Crowding, gradients, drop shadows, and starbursts destroy the positioning faster than anything else.
  2. Never show people's faces in SDLR imagery. No people, no figures, no pets — in stills. (Video is the exception; see §7.)
  3. Never put a price floor in public copy. Per-service investment bands in a table are fine. A "starting at" number is not.

Where the master files live

Logos, print-ready artwork, the image library, and templates are in the client folder under 02 Clients/san-diego-landscape-remodeling/assets/. Ask Gio for access. The live website — the most reliable reference for the brand in practice — is sandiegolandscaperemodeling.com.

02

The company

You can't design for a brand you can't describe. Here is the whole thing in one page.

What SDLR is

A boutique outdoor-living design-build firm covering San Diego County. SDLR designs and builds complete outdoor environments — hardscape, outdoor kitchens, fire features, pergolas, pools, lighting, drainage — as one coordinated project, under one Class B general contractor license, with one crew and one schedule.

What SDLR is not

Not a landscaper. Not a lawn or maintenance service. Not a tree company. Not an emergency contractor. SDLR does not do same-day work, does not price-match, and does not take small-scope jobs. Any design or copy that makes SDLR look like a general-purpose yard service is off-brand, regardless of how well it's executed.

"By design, not by dispatch."

This is the governing positioning line. Projects are planned months ahead and executed deliberately. Scarcity and intention are the product. Nothing you make should signal speed, availability, or urgency.

Who it's for

The buyer

Grandparents and young families. Household income $600K+, home value $8M+. Professionals, owners, investors. They are quality-shopping, not price-shopping. They want it done right, once.

What they're actually buying

Pride when family comes over. A space they genuinely use. In Gio's words — "a subtle wealth statement that makes a big statement when people come over." Not Instagram content. Lived experience.

Their fears (what good work quietly answers)

  1. The contractor blows the budget
  2. It takes far longer than promised
  3. It never gets finished
  4. The workmanship looks bad in two years
  5. Nobody communicates

Calm, precise, well-made collateral is itself the answer to all five. Sloppy work signals a sloppy build.

Known issue — fix before any new print run

The vector file SDLR.svg carries older color values — sage #556B5D and gold #C9A96A — which do not match the current brand palette (#4E5D4A / #BDA572). The hex values in §4 of this guide are canonical; they are what the website and the approved 2026 yard signs use. If you open the SVG, recolor it to the §4 values before output, and send the corrected file back to Gio so the master can be replaced.

Clear space and minimum size

  • Clear space: on all four sides, leave at least the height of the "S" in the SDLR monogram. Nothing enters that zone — no type, no rules, no photo edges, no page trim.
  • Minimum width: 110 px on screen, 1 inch in print, for the stacked lockup. Below that the two-line wordmark fills in and stops being legible — use the monogram alone instead.
  • Monogram alone (SDLR, no wordmark) is permitted for favicons, social avatars, embroidery, and any application under the minimum. It is never the primary mark on a first impression.

Do

  • Scale proportionally, always
  • Use one solid brand color per lockup
  • Place it on calm, uncluttered areas of a photo
  • Let it sit small and confident — it does not need to be big

Don't

  • Stretch, skew, rotate, or arch it
  • Add drop shadows, outer glows, bevels, or gradients
  • Recolor it outside the palette, or use a metallic gradient "gold"
  • Put it on a busy photo without a scrim
  • Rebuild the wordmark in a different typeface
  • Enclose it in a box or badge that isn't in this guide
  • Pair it with a second logo inside the clear-space zone

How to mix them

  • Roughly 70 / 20 / 8 / 2 — paper, sage, ink, gold. Gold is a seasoning, not an ingredient.
  • Gold never carries body text. It fails contrast on cream and looks cheap at small sizes. Gold is for hairline rules, uppercase eyebrows on a dark field, and the logo.
  • Dark bands are sage-800 (#2E382B) or sage (#4E5D4A) with paper text. Never black-on-white blocks.
  • Pure white is not in the palette. If you need a lift above paper, use white only as a card surface inside a paper field — never as the page background.

Accessibility floors

  • Ink on paper — 15.1:1. Use freely.
  • Paper on sage — 7.2:1. Use freely.
  • Sage-700 on paper — 8.6:1. Safe for small text.
  • Gold on paper — 2.1:1. Fails. Rules and large display only, never body copy or small UI text.
  • Gold on sage-800 — 5.4:1. Passes for text at 14px+. This is the correct way to use gold type.

Montserrat — logo only

The wordmark under the monogram is set in Montserrat. That is a historical artifact of the logo artwork, not a brand typeface. Never set new type in Montserrat. If you need the wordmark at a new size, scale the vector — don't retype it.

Rules

  • Headlines are serif. Everything else is sans. No exceptions, and never the reverse.
  • Sentence case for headlines, not Title Case. Uppercase is reserved for eyebrows and buttons, where the 0.18em tracking does the work.
  • The eyebrow is the signature move. A tiny tracked-out uppercase label above a serif headline, often with a 58px gold hairline between them. It appears on nearly every SDLR surface. Use it.
  • One measure, always. Body copy caps at about 38rem (roughly 70 characters). Long full-width lines break the editorial feel instantly.
  • No italics for emphasis in body copy. Cormorant italic is for a pull quote or a single considered word. Inter italic, essentially never.
  • Never justify text. Ragged right, always.
  • Real punctuation. Em dashes, curly quotes, proper ellipses. This is a detail buyers at this level register without naming it.

Layout & graphic elements

The SDLR layout language is a short list. There are no other decorative elements — if it isn't here, it isn't in the brand.

The gold hairline

A 1px gold rule, 58–64px wide, sitting between an eyebrow and a headline, or centered under a section title. The single most recognizable SDLR graphic element.

The eyebrow stack

Outdoor Living
A space that feels complete

Label, rule, serif headline. The default way to open any section, page, slide, or print panel.

Spacing and structure

  • Generous vertical rhythm. Sections breathe at 80–128px on desktop. Crowding is the fastest way to lose the register.
  • Three container widths: narrow (38rem, for reading), wide (52rem, for mixed content), editorial (72rem, for full layouts).
  • Alternating bands carry the rhythm: paper → dark sage → paper → warm paper. Dark bands are for philosophy, process, and quotes — the moments that aren't selling.
  • Squared corners. Buttons, cards, and image frames have no border radius. Rounded corners read consumer-app, not architectural.
  • Hairline borders only — 1px, in #E5DECA on light or rgba(245,241,232,.2) on dark. No heavy strokes, no boxes-within-boxes.

Buttons

Book a Consultation View the Work

Solid sage primary, outlined secondary. Square, uppercase, tracked 0.18em, generous padding. On dark backgrounds use the ghost variant — paper outline, transparent fill.

The CTA language is fixed

The primary call to action is "Book a Consultation." Never "Get a Free Estimate," "Free Quote," "Call Today," or anything with an exclamation point. For a $20–75K decision, positioning beats friction-reduction — "free" cheapens the thing being offered.

Do

  • Let one idea own a page or a frame
  • Use cream negative space as a deliberate element
  • Align to a visible grid; keep edges honest
  • Let photography be the loudest thing present

Don't

  • Gradients, glows, bevels, or drop shadows
  • Rounded corners or pill shapes
  • Icon sets, badges, starbursts, or ribbons
  • Stock "luxury" flourishes — laurels, crowns, script accents
  • Text over the busy part of a photo without a scrim
  • More than one accent color on a surface
  • Centered body copy (centered headlines are fine)
07

Photography & imagery

The imagery is the product. Everything else in this guide supports it. The standard is architectural editorial — Architectural Digest, Dwell, an Aman resort's website.

Rule one: a real photo beats a render, every time

Anything that has actually been built gets a real photograph. A phone in good side light beats any generated image. Capturing 15–20 real frames is part of every job closeout.

Generated imagery is reserved for what doesn't exist yet: concepts, unbuilt designs, a client's before-and-after vision, and gaps in the library. Never place a raw render next to a real photo in the same grid, carousel, or spread — the real one exposes the fake one instantly.

The website portfolio is stricter than social

The site's portfolio and "our work" sections use genuine project photography only — never generated images presented as a catalog of completed builds. Six real projects beat fifty renderings. The social feed is treated as a styled showroom; the website portfolio is a verified record.

The look

  • One dominant light direction with real falloff — sun raking across travertine, dappled pergola shadows. Never softly lit from everywhere.
  • Real dynamic range. Let skies and speculars roll off to white; let shadows go genuinely dark. "Evenly visible everywhere" is the tell that something is fake.
  • Warm-neutral, restrained color. Magazine warmth. No teal push, no orange push, no heavy saturation.
  • A real focal plane. Shallow depth — a styled counter sharp, the background soft.
  • Styled and lived in — real firewood, ceramics, a throw left slightly undone. Not empty and pristine.
  • Architectural framing — straight level verticals, no keystone, eye-level or slightly elevated, 24mm or 35mm.

Hard rules — these are not preferences

  1. No people, no faces, ever. No figures, no body parts, no pets. The spaces are shot like an architectural feature before the owners arrive.
  2. Show the neighbors. Ninety-nine percent of SDLR jobs sit on a normal San Diego lot. Every image shows neighboring rooflines, second stories, or walls over the back fence. A beautiful house alone in empty hills is rejected on sight.
  3. Vary the house every single time. Rotate architecture (Spanish, modern, ranch, craftsman, coastal contemporary, farmhouse) and materials. The same white-stucco house repeating is the biggest giveaway there is.
  4. It must look like San Diego — SoCal architecture, California planting (olive, agave, succulents, citrus, palms, bougainvillea). No maples, oaks, or Midwest skies. And match the specific neighborhood named: no ocean behind an El Cajon home, no sprawling estate on a North Park lot.
  5. One photograph per image. No collages. No split screens, no grids, no three-in-one. Before/after is the only permitted two-frame format, and only when that's the explicit intent.
  6. No text, logos, or watermarks baked into the image. Overlays are a layout decision, made separately.
  7. Name real products. Every image is built around materials SDLR actually installs, so when someone asks "what pavers are those?" there's a real answer. See the list below.

Subject rotation

Vary widely so the work speaks to every buyer: putting greens, sport courts, pools and spas, zen retreat yards, full entertainment yards, low-maintenance yards for retirees, family yards with turf and fire pits, small urban yards done beautifully — and driveways and front-yard curb appeal, which must recur and have historically been skipped. Occasional drone and aerial frames show scale nothing else can.

08

Video & motion

Premium comes from planning, light, and designed frames — not from captions, effects, or cutting speed.

Two registers

Clean — anyone talking to camera

Locked-off or tightly controlled camera, properly lit (key + fill, or window + bounce), clean background, bright, almost perfect. As few cuts as possible — a lit single take with punch-ins beats a jump-cut chain. Polish comes from pre-planned graphic elements, not captions over everything. Mike at job sites is shot in this register.

Warm — crews, process, materials

Cinematic and warmer. Handheld with intent, shallow depth, golden highlights, texture close-ups of hands, tools, and materials. Wide crew shot → tool close-up → hands detail, repeated. Music carries it; no VO, no captions.

Rhythm

  • Let it breathe. Brand films and interview pieces: b-roll holds 4–8s, a hero frame can hold 8–10s, with music-only air between thoughts. Fewer bites, each given room.
  • Social montage: 3–5s per shot, occasional 1s accents and 6–8s holds. No sawtooth pacing; change pace only at a story turn.
  • Never cut on the word. Every dialogue bite starts at least 0.3s before the first word and runs 0.6s past the last, to the natural breath. Joins get an audio crossfade.
  • Cut on action or on the beat. Never on dead air.

Captions

  • Short-form 9:16 with someone talking: captions always on.
  • Style: bold uppercase sans, word-by-word or 2–4 word cards, one color, lower third, inside safe zones (above the bottom 35%, inside the side 6%).
  • No keyword color highlighting, no pill backgrounds, no emoji, no bounce. The September 2026 SDLR reels — gold-highlight, mixed-case, pill backgrounds — are the counter-example, not a reference.
  • Montage with no one speaking: no captions. Text only as a title card or designed label.
  • Long-form 16:9: no captions. Text only as title cards and inserts.

Image quality — non-negotiable

  • Every frame sharp and full-resolution. Soft, upscaled, over-compressed, or noisy low-light footage is disqualifying, not "fixable in post."
  • Exposure, white balance, and contrast matched across every shot before any look is applied. A conversion LUT alone is not a grade. LUT strength ≤50% on balanced footage.
  • Pick the steadiest window of a take, not the first usable one. Stabilized clips must actually read as steady — otherwise replace the clip.
  • People are in frame on purpose or not at all. Nobody wandering through the back of a shot.
  • Grain or an aged look only as a deliberate, named choice — never as cover for soft footage.

Sound & finishing

  • Music first. Choose the track before the paper edit and cut to it. A cut built without its music is a shot list, not a rough cut.
  • Music ducked 6–12dB under voice, room tone under every gap. Deliver −14 LUFS / −1 dBTP.
  • Straight cuts. Dip to black at section changes only. No transition packs, no speed ramps unless the source is ≥60fps and it serves the story, no particle or "cinematic" overlays.
  • Endings are deliberate: a held hero frame, a logo + tagline card, or a hard stop on the beat. No trailing talk.
  • Deliver 1080×1920 for 9:16 at high bitrate, mastered at the highest resolution available. Never a re-encode of a re-encode.
  • Naming: sdlr-<piece>-<ratio>-v<N>-<date>.mp4. Keep every version.

Planning is part of editing

Every piece starts with a one-page plan before shooting: the formal idea, the hook frame, a shot list by register, the graphic elements, the music direction, and the ending. If the idea can't be named in five words, the piece isn't planned yet. The hook lands inside 1.5 seconds.

Voice & copy

Editorial-luxury with human warmth. Ultra-premium positioning, with real people clearly present behind it. Think an Aman resort's website, not a landscaping company's.

Personality

Premium. Intentional. Confident. Grounded. The quiet confidence of a firm that does excellent work and knows it — without arrogance and without a pitch. Less contractor, more trusted expert who has done this a hundred times.

  • Premium but never pretentious
  • Confident but never pushy
  • Educational but never condescending
  • Design-aware, but speaks like a builder — not an interior designer

Phrases to lean on

"Built with intention. Not rushed."

"A space that feels complete"

"Designed around how you actually live"

"Structure before landscaping"

"A natural extension of your home"

"Outdoor living, not just outdoor space"

Craft

  • Short, confident sentences. Let the work speak.
  • Lead with the outcome or the feeling, not the service list.
  • Reach for: intentional, cohesive, refined, structured, built, transformed, designed, realized.
  • Reference the San Diego lifestyle naturally — indoor/outdoor living, year-round use.
  • These clients are making a real investment. Write to them accordingly.

Banned — rewrite on sight

  • "dream backyard"
  • "transform your space"
  • "your vision, our expertise"
  • "free estimate" / "free quote"
  • "cheap" / "affordable" / "budget-friendly"
  • "best prices" / "price match"
  • "quality work" / "quality guaranteed"
  • "best in the business"
  • "we do it all"
  • "act now" / "limited spots" / "don't wait"
  • "same-day" / "immediate" / "we can be there tomorrow"
  • "call us today!"
  • "lawn care" / "gardening" / "maintenance"
  • "our studio" / "visit our showroom" / "drop by"
  • Real-estate-listing clichés
  • Any exclamation point in brand copy

The pricing rule — hard, and easy to break by accident

No price floor appears in any public prose. Not "projects from $20K," not "starting at," not "minimum investment of," not the figure written out in words. On editorial-luxury copy a concrete floor reads like a directory listing — and it underprices SDLR to exactly the buyer worth winning.

What is allowed: per-service investment bands inside a services table (e.g. "Retaining & Seating Walls · $20K–$100K"). Those are context for someone already serious.

Use scope language instead of numbers to filter: "small-scope work," "full backyard remodels," "design-build at this scale." Or register cues: "boutique," "a committed investment," "one honest number."

Naming rules — hard

  • Never name Mike's other companies in public copy. If depth must be referenced: "a specialty materials background" — descriptive, never branded.
  • Never name the Field Lead by full name publicly. Use "our Field Lead" or "our lead craftsman." His 25+ years and role can be cited; the name cannot.
  • Gio and Mike's first names are fine in public copy.
  • The La Mesa address is an office, not a studio. It appears in the NAP block only. Never "visit our studio."

Honesty guardrails — legal, not stylistic

  • No testimonials until a real SDLR client consents in writing. The standing placeholder is "References available on request."
  • No fabricated job claims. No invented client names, no "we just finished this for the ___ family," no fake before/afters tied to a real address. Confident, present-tense description of the design and build is fine; a manufactured delivered-project story is false advertising for a licensed contractor.
  • All "licensed" claims cite Mike's Class B license number. Gio is not a licensed contractor — never imply otherwise.
  • Never offer in-person visits to completed projects. Client privacy: their homes never become a showroom.

Pools — get this right

Two design tiers — never conflate them

Free concept visualization — a photoreal render of the client's own backyard, made from photos taken on the property walk. It is a visualization, not an engineered plan. No exact dimensions, no elevations. In client-facing copy this is a "concept render" — never "a design."

Paid true design ($2,500–$6,000+) — exact dimensions, elevations, fully editable. The words design, the plan, exact dimensions, elevations belong to this tier only. Promising the paid tier for free over-promises the lead and cheapens the design.

Pre-flight checklist

Run this before anything ships. If a line fails, it isn't ready — regardless of deadline.

Before you send it

  1. Could this be mistaken for a landscaping company? If yes, start over.
  2. Palette check. Only sage, gold, paper, ink and their tints. No fifth color. No pure white background. No pure black.
  3. Type check. Serif headlines, sans everything else. Eyebrows tracked 0.18em. Nothing set in Montserrat.
  4. Gold check. Gold is carrying rules and accents only — not body text on cream.
  5. Imagery check. No faces. Neighbors visible. The house differs from the last piece. Reads as San Diego. No collage. No baked-in text.
  6. Mixing check. No raw render sitting beside a real photo in the same set.
  7. Copy check. No banned phrases. No price floor in prose. No exclamation points. CTA reads "Book a Consultation."
  8. Claims check. No testimonials without written consent. No invented project stories. License claims cite CSLB #1139785.
  9. Facts check. NAP matches §10 character for character.
  10. Craft check. Real punctuation, honest alignment, generous space, square corners, hairline borders.
  11. Print only: bleed set, proof pulled, sage and gold matched by eye to this guide.
